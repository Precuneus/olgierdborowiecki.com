Train yourself with AI

An AI model is a reader you can practice real communication skills on. Where it gets you wrong, a person might too, and you see it before a colleague or a student has to say so. When I teach, I need to know what my students already know. When I prompt a model, I need to engineer the context. Each disappointing answer tells you what to add to that context next time. That is the step from chatting with AI to building systems with it.

The gap

A language model is trained on much of what people have written. Asked cold, it tends to give the most typical answer, which I call the median of the internet. It knows only what you tell it about your work, the instructions and files you hand it. When its answer comes back generic or off target, the problem is usually in what you gave it. Ask it to make your email “more professional” and it may strip out the warmth you meant to keep, because the warmth stayed in your head.

Thinking on your thinking

Getting a disappointing answer from a model is feedback for your future prompts. This means you need to think on your own thinking to structure the message you want to communicate. Like with other people, who need information accounting for the background knowledge.

Thinking on your own thinking is what psychologists call metacognition, knowing what you know and what you only assume. I think of the next step as metacognition turned outward. You ask what the other side already knows, a machine or a person. You are building a model of someone else's model.

From chatting to building

It is on me, and on you, to keep learning how to communicate with other people and with AI. It is a process for life, and you can always get better at taking the other side's view. A good conversation with a model can take me hours of hard, deliberate work. Most of it goes into deciding what the model needs to know.

This work is the difference between chatting with AI and building agentic systems. Building starts when you read an answer for what it lacks and add the missing piece to the model's context. The next conversation starts further ahead. The model has the missing piece, and you know what you tend to leave out, with models and with people.
